# Chapter 12: Know Where the Brain Stops — Privacy Tiers, the Critic Step, and the Capability Cliff Audit

The boundary of what stays yours is not discovered once — it's a maintained line that moves as the tools move, the clients move, and your judgment sharpens or softens. Draw it in ink, revisit it in daylight, redraw it quarterly.

## Privacy tiers (draw the line before you cross it)

Classify every data type you'd feed an AI by sensitivity before you start feeding anything:

- **Tier 1 — open**: public material, published work, general research. Feed freely.
- **Tier 2 — internal**: drafts, outlines, non-sensitive client context, your own notes. Feed with care; know the provider's data policy.
- **Tier 3 — restricted**: personal data, financials, confidential client info, anything under NDA or regulation. Keep out of consumer AI tools by default; use approved/enterprise channels or process locally.

The tiering is done once, up front, so a 10 p.m. rush job never becomes an accidental Tier-3 upload.

## The critic step

Every output gets a second look — the confidence sub-chain from Chapter 6 is the mechanism:
1. Draft produced by the chain.
2. Confidence self-grade + flagging of uncertain/missing parts.
3. Human critic step: accept, patch, or rerun.

Case: *Mata v. Avianca* (S.D.N.Y. 2023) — lawyers filed a brief containing six fabricated case citations generated by an AI chatbot they never verified. Sanctioned. The failure wasn't the hallucination; it was the missing critic step. Case: "the confident lie" — output delivered wrong because it *sounded* complete.

## The never-fully-outsource list

Some functions, fully outsourced, dissolve the judgment clients actually pay for:

1. **Planned recommendations** — AI informs; you own the answer that changes a client's direction.
2. **Client relationships + emotional reading** — outsource the draft, keep the timing (send today or Tuesday is yours).
3. **Final approvals** — your name on the work is an implicit warranty; "the AI did it" is not a professional answer.
4. **Ethical and boundary decisions** — conflict checks, scope you can stand behind, pushing back on a brief that feels off. Character tasks. AI has no character.
5. **Your unique angle and voice** — the more you outsource your point of view, the more your work converges on the model's median. Clients notice as "your recent work feels different," which usually means worse.

Write your own list in the front of your project notebook and revisit it. The point is the conscious act of deciding what stays yours.

## The quarterly capability cliff audit

Capability cliffs are where a task transitions between human-only and AI-viable (or vice versa). Most solopreneurs audit their AI use approximately never — leaving capability on the table or running silently degraded workflows.

**Quarterly ritual: 90 minutes, blank document, walk every workflow, three questions:**

1. *What am I doing manually that might now be delegable?* (last quarter's model couldn't hold multi-document analysis; this quarter's can.)
2. *What am I delegating that I should take back or restructure?* (the 8-month-old summarization chain may now produce shallower output; the critic catches individual errors, the audit catches systemic drift.)
3. *What changed in my client work that changes the boundary?* (a regulated-industry client shifts tier boundaries; personal-data projects open new constraints.)

Rate each workflow: AI output better / same / worse than last quarter. Dana bakes this into her quarterly business review and has caught two cliff edges this way — a summarization tool that quietly lost long-document handling, and a drafting tool that suddenly got dramatically better at her exact use case. Knowing your cliffs means you climb them on purpose instead of being quietly shaped by them.
