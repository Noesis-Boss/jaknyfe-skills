The prompt is self-contained — no workspace files, memory, or tools needed. Reasoning alone, per the creative-derailment guardrail.

## Story 1: the hire she never made

Maya stared at the org-chart template on her screen, cursor blinking over the second box. The top box read *Maya Okonkwo — Founder & Strategy*. The second box was empty. She'd downloaded the template six months ago from a "scaling your consultancy" blog, and that second box had been empty every day since. It was supposed to say *Research Analyst*.

She couldn't afford one. She knew that. The 9:00 AM Tuesday call with the municipal utility client didn't know that.

"Your carbon-reduction framework is thin," the procurement director had said on the last call. "We need three comparable cities, their actual spend, and their twelve-month outcomes. Can your team get that to us by Thursday?"

*My team.* Maya almost laughed.

She opened a blank document and started writing. Not a brief — something denser. *You are a municipal energy research analyst. When given a city name and year, retrieve: program budget, vendor list, kWh reduced, and cost per kWh. Format as a four-row table. Cite sources. Flag any number older than two years.* She pasted it into the AI tool she'd been using like a glorified search bar for eight months and typed beneath it: *Columbus, Ohio, 2023.*

When she came back from refilling her coffee, there was a table. Three of four cities were solid. The fourth — she'd plugged in Austin — had a gap, and the model had flagged it. A red note: *2023 figures not publicly reported. 2022 available. Re-run?*

She'd never gotten that from herself at 11 PM.

Thursday, she sent the deck. The procurement director replied in two hours: "Thorough. We'd like to talk scope."

Maya closed the org-chart template and typed two words into the empty box: *Research — Staff.*

It wasn't a hire. But for the first time, it wasn't an empty box.

## Story 2: the cleaner's question

Derek ran his copywriting studio from a kitchen table that held two laptops and a cold cup of rooibos. His assistant, Priya — actual human, part-time, wonderful — had just quit to finish her MBA. That left 14 active client briefs and nobody to research them.

"You use that AI thing, yeah?" his cleaner asked on her way out, nodding at the glowing screen. "My nephew does something with that. Says it's like having someone in the laptop."

Derek almost dismissed it. Then he thought about the stack of briefs.

He opened a brand-new doc and wrote what Priya's onboarding file had never contained: a real procedure. *"You are a competitive-brand research analyst for a copywriter. For each client, deliver: (1) top 3 competitor taglines, (2) one paragraph on their claimed positioning, (3) the gap their messaging leaves open, (4) a one-liner our client could own. Do not invent quotes. If you can't find a real tagline, say 'Not found.'"*

The first two came back gold. The third — a pet-food brand in Oregon — came back with "Not found" on all three taglines. He leaned forward. Under normal circumstances he'd have brainstormed three clever ones and moved on. The model's empty fields made him go look.

The competitors hadn't launched yet. The entire category was open ground. The brief he wrote that afternoon was the best of his career.

He looked at his prompt and said out loud what he hadn't told anyone: "You're actually better with rules than Priya was."

The laptop didn't answer. But the next morning, it had researched three more clients overnight.

## Story 3: the spreadsheet he refused to open

Gloria emailed the spreadsheet at 6:14 AM. Eighty-four rows. Each a class participant she'd coached in the last nine months. David, who built her online course backend, wanted to "do a cohort analysis." She'd rather teach a third weekend workshop.

She didn't want the spreadsheet open. She wanted the answer inside it.

She pulled up the AI tab and typed a long instruction she would never have written a month ago. *"You are a coaching engagement analyst. Review the attached participant list. Group by stated goal at intake (career pivot, burnout recovery, skill sharpening). For each group report: average weeks active, most common drop-off module, and one quote per group — pull only from the open 'notes' column. Do not invent quotes. If a note column is blank, write 'no note.'"*

She hit enter, walked to the kettle, and by the time the water boiled she had three clean sections. The burnout group — her biggest — stalled hardest at Module 6: *Rebuilding the Calendar.* Thirty-one of thirty-eight left notes. Two patterns surfaced in the quotes she'd scrawled weeks ago. She'd forgotten all of them.

She called David. "I don't need the cohort analysis. I need to redesign Module 6."

The spreadsheet stayed closed. The conversation that mattered opened.

She saved the prompt with the filename `cohort-analyst.md` and realized, mid-click, that she had just written the first job description of her life — for someone who would never collect a paycheck.

## Story 4: the word he refused to say

The prompt was beautiful. Sujin had written it at 6 AM, after coffee and before shame. *"You are a lead-generation research analyst. Given a target sector, return 20 companies meeting: 5–25 employees, Series A or later, founded after 2019, hiring for [role] within 90 days. Prioritize those with a stated mission affecting small businesses. Format as a table."*

It returned 20 rows in 9 minutes. He checked four at random. Three were real, one had been acquired and the headcount was wrong. He instructed the prompt to add: *"Verify current employee count via LinkedIn. Flag acquisitions with status: ACQUIRED."*

Rerun. Clean.

His business partner Frank watched over Zoom. "Who found these?"

"The research analyst."

Frank squinted. "We don't have one."

Sujan focused on a spreadsheet. A nail on his ring finger tapped the trackpad. He mumbled something.

"Come again?"

"The AI."

"Oh. Why not say so?"

"Because," Sujan said, and stopped. The real reason was interesting and he wasn't ready to say it. Three years ago he'd paid a contractor $2,400 to produce a smaller version of the exact same list, slower, with worse hit rates. The reason he wouldn't say "the AI did it" was not that the AI was wrong. It was that saying it out loud made him feel like a founder who had stopped hiring humans.

Two days later he paid a junior researcher $28 an hour to do what the analyst did, and the analyst found the mistakes. He raised his eyebrows at himself in the bathroom mirror the next morning, said "OK" to no one, and updated the org-chart tile on his wall.

## Story 5: the bathroom tile problem

After her second bathroom renovation, Wendy had learned one thing: tile setters don't talk to plumbers. So when her tiny design studio started getting offers to design three café interiors in one quarter, she knew she needed research before the mood boards. What palette was trending in that neighborhood? Which competitor had already won work there?

She had no associate. She didn't want one.

She wrote a single-page document and titled it `research-analyst.md`. *"You are an interiors-and-trends research analyst. Given a neighborhood and café concept, deliver: (1) three recently opened cafés in the area with their stated design references, (2) the common material palette if discoverable, (3) the unserved design niche. If a design reference cannot be confirmed, say 'unverified.' Do not invent neighborhood names."*

She ran it for Bed-Stuy and *brunch-forward*. The response cited two real cafés she'd missed entirely and one design niche (quiet interiors without live plants — two competitors' "jungle walls" had been magazined to death). The third café cited — "can't confirm" — she appreciated. She'd seen too many junior designers invent a source just to fill the column.

She sent her proposal to the client with a single line: *I've reviewed the neighborhood recently; dark wood and sound control is open territory.* The client signed the next morning.

Walking home she thought: she hadn't hired anyone. But she'd written, for the second time in her life, a brief so specific a stranger could do the work. The first time had been her briefs to tile setters. The difference was that this stranger was inside her laptop, worked at 11 PM, and never once asked "is this what you meant?"

## Story 6: the voice she already knew

The third time the board interrupted her, Tara's voice went quiet and stayed there.

She'd told them the talent-survey data she'd gathered was "based on interviews." It was. She'd conducted 41 calls herself over six months and typed up the notes. The analyst — her written prompt — had synthesized them into themes.

"You said 'synthesis.' That's a fancy word for the machine rearranging your own notes," the board chair said.

Nothing on her face moved. "Yes. That's exactly what it is. I write the notes; the analyst surfaces patterns I miss because I'm tired. The themes are mine. The pattern-matching is faster."

"Whose voice is in the report? Yours or the AI's?"

She sat for a beat. "Mine. I read the output and rewrite where it doesn't sound like me. The analyst is a research tool with a rule: never invent a quote, never fabricate a statistic, and always mark inference. I keep the rule because I can't afford to be wrong with a board."

The chair leaned back. "OK. Show me the rule next time."

She'd already saved it as `analyst-charter.md` — three rules, 80 words. Boring. Crucial. She'd spent more time on that charter than on any slide in the deck.

That evening she rewrote the section of the report that had bothered her — not the analyst's wording, the underlying logic — and shipped the revision without a call. The chair replied at 6:11 PM: "Good."

The building was silent when the email landed. She made soup.

## Story 7: the org chart with no salary line

Marcus had drawn his org chart on the back of a Whole Foods receipt at the gym. It wasn't a business exercise; it was an emotional one. Two boxes: *Me* on top, *Research* on the bottom. The line between them — he labeled it *"Revenue"*. Because that was what every hire was, in his mental model. A line that cost money and needed producing above.

He pulled out his phone and wrote into the AI: *"You are a competitive research analyst serving a one-person marketing consultancy. For a given client industry, surface: (1) three emerging sub-niches, (2) the current dominant narrative in that niche, (3) the gap a small client could own. If you cannot confirm a niche is emerging, label it 'unconfirmed.' Do not invent niche names."*

The next morning he ran it for *independent bookstores* — a client niche he'd been circling. The dominant narrative came back instantly: *community hubs*. The gap came back instantaneously: *no store within 20 miles of the client has a Tolkien-specific reading circle.* He verified. It was true.

He pitched the client at 9 AM. They signed at 11.

He went back to the receipt, drew a third box under Research, labeled it *"Clone"*. Smiled. Threw it away on the way out and rewrote it carefully in ink in the back of his Moleskine with one addition — a note below *Clone*: *"No salary line. Just structure."*

The Moleskine went in his bag. The bus pulled away. He didn't feel busy; he felt staffed.

## Story 8: the late night he stopped having

It was 12:14 AM and Beth, for the first time in two years, was not at her desk doing the next morning's research.

This was disorienting. She was a proposal writer for nonprofits. Twelve million in grants secured for clients across Phoenix in the last eight years. Theire promising lists — the "funder scan" — was always the late-night task: foundation X's giving shift, the family office opening a new cycle, the federal notice buried on a Thursday. Every proposal opened with a scanned landscape. The scanning was the part that took until 1 AM.

Two weeks earlier she'd written a one-page document she titled `funder-analyst.md`. *"You are a philanthropic research analyst for a grant proposal consultancy. Given a client mission and geography, return: (1) five foundations whose current giving cycle overlaps the client's mission, (2) the deadline for each, (3) a sentence on the foundation's stated 2024–2025 priority, and (4) the gap between the foundation's interest and what the client currently lists. Flag any deadline under 14 days. Do not invent deadlines. If a foundation's 2024–25 priority is not published, write 'priority not declared.'"*

She ran it at 4 PM, edited the output at 4:45, and the proposal team had the landscape before they went home. She'd corrected three fabrications in a first test run — the model had guessed deadlines. The instruction *"Do not invent deadlines"* had fixed it.

Now it was 12:14 and her kitchen was clean and her textbook was open. She went and stood by the window and thought about the silence. The second box on her chart had been empty for years: *Researcher*. Tonight it wasn't empty.

She went to bed at a reasonable hour. In the morning she would call what she'd built "my analyst" without flinching, because the work it did was real, and the rules it followed were hers.
