**1. The Note That Survived Eleven Relocations**

Maya kept the sticky note. Yellow, curled at the corner, stuck to the edge of her monitor since March. "Call Renata about partnership terms." Nine months it had survived. Four monitor replacements. Two office moves. The ink had faded to a ghost of itself.

She found it again while hunting for a client tax ID. Touched it. Didn't act. Pushed it onto a new sticky because the old one had lost its adhesive.

Renata called in December. She had partnered with someone else. "I reached out three times this year," Renata said. "Didn't you see my emails?"

The sticky note had been touched eleven times. Decision debt: a full year of lost revenue from a single deferred triage. That night, Maya shredded the note and opened a blank document. At the top she wrote: *Touch it twice without deciding, die.*

She ran her first triage pass. Forty-seven open notes. Thirty were already dead. She dropped them all in under six minutes, the relief physical—a pressure she hadn't named releasing from her sternum.

**2. The Vendor Portal That Ate Tuesday**

Gary's consulting dashboard had seventeen open vendor tickets. Not a crisis. Just a hum. Each ticket touched, glanced at, deferred. The portal pinged him at 9 AM each Monday. By 10 AM, he had re-opened four of them, changed nothing, closed the tab.

This Tuesday, the portal went down for maintenance. Four hours. He could not check it. He could not defer.

Without the portal's permission to feel busy, he saw what the tickets actually were. Fourteen were auto-renewals that needed an "acknowledge" click. One was a real contract dispute. Two were spam.

He batched the fourteen acknowledgements into a single five-minute pass. Dropped the spam. Opened the contract dispute in a separate doc and wrote a three-paragraph response to the vendor.

The portal returned at 2 PM. He did not log in.

**3. Route to Client**

Deandra's client, Lewis, kept sending her articles. "Thought you'd find this useful," he'd write. She printed them. Filed them. Maybe read one in three.

She invoiced Lewis monthly. The articles felt likeinvisible labor, unpaid intake.

In September she renamed a folder *Lewis / Routed.* Every article he sent got triaged there within twenty-four hours. No reading. No acknowledgment. The folder was his substrate, not hers.

Lewis was delighted to receive the monthly digest. The revelation became a service offering: she'd triage their information firehose, killing the mental cost of "should I read this" entirely.

**4. The Nightly Pass**

Jules worked until midnight most Thursdays. The Friday pass saved him.

2. *For every Act item, draft the next action in under ten words.*
3. *Flag any item appearing three times this week without progress — surface it as a decision debt alert.*

Thursday night outputs arrived in his inbox at 10:14. Friday morning he opened it with coffee.

Three items were flagged. One was a client proposal he had deferred six times. It would have died silently at year-end. He saw it now, sitting there like an unpaid bill, and felt the small electric jolt of decision awaiting.

He responded to the client by 10 AM. The client signed by Friday close. Years of deferred triage had cost him more than he could calculate. The nightly pass had cost him fourteen minutes.

**5. The Senior Strategist Who Couldn't Drop Anything**

Walter, a brand strategist at fifty-one, kept everything. Whiteboard photos, meeting notes, client brainstorm audios. "I'll process those this weekend," he told his wife every Sunday.

The weekends came and went.

His assistant, Pia, watched him drown. She watched a specifically promising client leave—the call went to voicemail because he was re-reading a six-month-old recording, convinced it contained a missed insight. It didn't.

One morning she wore her coat to his office and said, plainly, "Walter. You're holding inputs you decided not to decide about. That's the leak."

He stared. She continued. "Send me every note you haven't touched in three months, and I will tell you with ninety percent confidence it's dead. You can prove me wrong by acting on it within the week."

He sent her one hundred and forty-seven items. Pia returned sixty-two as *dead.* Walter challenged four. She was right about three.

The release was audible. He exhaled like a man who had been holding a breath since winter.

**6. The Email That Belonged To Someone Else**

Tomasz received the email at 11 PM and did not sleep. The client vendor was asking about deliverable timelines—a question Tomasz's project manager, Yusuf, was supposed to own.

Tomasz drafted a response. Deleted it. Drafted another. Deleted that too.

At 1 AM he understood: the email did not belong to him. Not because it was beneath him—because routing it through himself created a second copy of the decision that already had an owner.

He forwarded the thread to Yusuf with one line: *Yours. Handle by 10 AM. I'm out of this loop.*

Yusuf responded at 8:47 AM. The client had their answer by 9:15.

Tomasz had spent the energy of four hours on an email whose owner had already been paid to answer it. The lesson was not loud. It was a quiet tightening in his chest: **inputs have homes. Leaving them at your desk doesn't make them yours.**

He started routing before reading. His unread count became, for the first time in years, a number he could look at directly.

**8. The Empty Substrate**

The morning after the great triage, Marisol's desk was clear. One notebook, one pen, one coffee. The whiteboard held four items: one Act, three routed.

She felt exposed. A cleared substrate should have felt like victory. Instead, it felt like standing in a room stripped of furniture, every footstep echoing.

She had confused input volume with importance for so long that an empty workspace registered as unemployment.

For two hours she questioned the purge. By noon she had drafted a client proposal she'd been deferring for six weeks. By 3 PM she had called her sister, something she'd postponed across two crisis cycles without noticing she had postponed it.

The substrate was not the work. The substrate was the debris between her and the work. Empty surface, full day. The math inverted what she'd believed: the pile hadn't been keeping her busy. It had been keeping her from beginning.

She closed the notebook, left the pen, and walked to the window. Four items on the whiteboard behind her. She would decide them by tomorrow.
