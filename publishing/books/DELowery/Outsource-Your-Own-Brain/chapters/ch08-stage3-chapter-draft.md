# Keep only the crown jewels

Every business has a vault. Most solopreneurs leave theirs wide open, then wonder why clients walk.

The vault holds your Crown Jewels—the irreplaceable work that justifies your invoice, cements client trust, and makes you *you*. Everything else occupies the setting: the goldsmith's bench, the velvet display, the security system. Necessary, but interchangeable. Replace the bench and a different craftsman still shines. Replace the jewels and you're selling empty boxes.

The solopreneurs who burn out, plateau, and eventually quit share a common failure: they delegate the wrong things, and cling to the wrong things. They outsource their crown jewels cheap or hoard the busywork out of guilt, and the result is the same—a business that can't survive them, or one that will eventually crush them.

This chapter teaches you how to separate the two, and how to delegate the rest without losing clients, quality, or sanity.

## The crown jewels test: what only you can do

Start with a single sheet of paper. List every task you performed last week—the client calls, the drafts, the invoicing, the Slack triage, the proposal polish, the research rabbit holes. All of it.

Now apply the Crown Jewels test to each line:

*Could a trained stranger do this to 80% of my quality without me in the room?*

If the answer is yes, it's not a crown jewel. It might be important work—critical to client outcomes even—but it's delegable. The crown jewels are the narrow set of activities where your *presence itself* is the deliverable.

For a consultant, your crown jewels are usually the diagnostic judgment—the moment you sit across from a client, absorb their messy context, and tell them the uncomfortable truth they're paying to hear. For a writer, it's the voice and the selection of what's actually worth saying. For a coach, it's the pattern recognition between sessions and the moment you decide to push back. For a designer, it's the editorial eye and the stakeholder translation.

Marta runs a six-figure strategy consultancy for mid-market SaaS companies. When I had her do this exercise, she listed 47 weekly tasks. Exactly five made it through the filter: the kickoff diagnostic, the executive readout, the scope-negotiation conversation, the final yes on deliverables, and one monthly thought-leadership piece that carried her voice into the market. Forty-two other tasks—research synthesis, slide production, competitive scans, scheduling, invoicing—were work she'd convinced herself was "strategic" because she was good at it.

Being good at something is not the same as being the only person who can do it. The Crown Jewels test is ruthless about that distinction.

Write your list now. Force yourself to cross off anything a competent hire or a trained AI assistant could handle to the 80% bar. Be honest. The survivors go on your never-delegate list later in this chapter. The rest is Swiss cheese.

## Swiss cheese, not fortress: how to open holes you can close

Most solopreneurs imagine delegation as building a fortress—walls, moat, gatehouse, all-or-nothing. Either you hand someone the keys or you don't. So they don't, because no one candidate passes every test, and the fortress stays sealed while they drown.

The better model is Swiss cheese. You don't need one perfect hole. You need a wheel with several openings carved out, each one sized to whoever fits it best. You can close any hole instantly if quality drops.

A few holes you can carve this week:

**Research and synthesis.** A trained assistant can pull competitors, summarize long PDFs, and assemble a first-pass evidence pack. You review the pack, cross out half of it, refine the rest. You're still making the call. You've stopped doing the find.

**Drafting scaffolding.** An AI assistant can generate outlines, section frameworks, and first-pass prose at a stated voice. Your job becomes sharpening, not starting from a blank page. For a writer, this is the difference between two hours and thirty minutes on a draft section.

**Scheduling, billing, client intake forms, project-status updates.** None of this is voice. None of this is judgment. All of it is delegate-able this week to a part-time VA for a few hundred dollars.

**Status meetings you don't need to be in.** A project manager—part-time, fractional, even a sharp VA—can run the standing check-in, then escalate only the decisions that need you. Most solopreneurs sit in meetings they should have delegates in, because they've never defined an escalation threshold.

James, a brand designer in Austin, carved three holes over a single weekend. He handed off inbox triage to a VA, swapped his moodboard research to an AI assistant feeding curated inspiration boards, and moved weekly client status calls to his project manager with a one-sentence escalation rule: "If the client is unhappy or we've slipped a date, pull me in." Two months later he'd reclaimed roughly ten hours a week and his clients reported *higher* responsiveness. He hadn't left any holes open—he'd carved precisely the ones he could close if needed, and he'd made himself the bottleneck only for the work that mattered.

The fortress mindset costs you because you can never *fully* trust anyone. The Swiss cheese mindset works because you don't have to. You trust each hole at its narrowest point, and you keep the authority to seal it back up.

One caveat: Swiss cheese still has structure. Without procedures, handoff collapses. Each hole requires a written checklist for what "done" looks like—length, format, sources, deadline—and a review step before the work touches a client. Two hours writing procedures saves twenty hours answering confused Slack messages.

## The never-Delegate list: client trust, taste, pricing, scope, and the final yes

Now the opposite file. Some tasks can theoretically be delegated and absolutely should not be. These are the crown jewels. They are small in time but enormous in leverage, and outsourcing them outsources the relationship that pays your invoices.

**Client trust conversations.** When a client is frustrated, confused, thinking about leaving, or about to expand scope, you take that call. No one else can read your client's emotional state through your history with them, and no one else should be making promises on your behalf. Delegate this and you've delegated the relationship.

**Taste calls on final deliverables.** An assistant can produce ten slide options. You pick the one going to the client. A designer I coached tried delegating final selection to a junior—two weeks later his biggest client quietly went elsewhere. Final taste is your signature. You can train judgment, but taste at the decision point is irreducibly yours.

**Pricing and scope negotiation.** Every dollar you let someone else negotiate is a dollar you'll eventually pay to recover. Pricing encodes your positioning and your confidence. A delegate will anchor lower than you, hedge harder, and concede sooner. Scope creep handled by anyone but you is scope creep you'll wear.

**The final yes on anything client-bound.** Before any artifact leaves the building—a report, a recommendation, a campaign, a redesigned landing page—your eyes are the last gate. Not a line-by-line edit necessarily, but a calibrated read for voice, quality, and client fit. Solopreneurs who skip this step eventually ship something that isn't theirs, and the client notices before you do.

**Anything that touches your name or reputation directly.** Speaking pitches, podcast appearances, thought-leadership posts, the bio on your site. You can have help with drafts and logistics, but the substance carries your voice and your judgment, and that voice is the asset clients are buying access to.

These five items together rarely occupy more than a quarter of your week. They are the most leveraged hours in your business. Guard them, protect them, and do them with full attention, uninterrupted—if necessary, by hiding the calendar from anyone who schedules you.

The test for any never-delegate item is simple: if this went wrong, would the client feel it as a breach of *trust*, not just quality? Quality slips can be fixed. Trust slips end relationships. Treat the trust surface as non-transferable.

Nadia, an executive coach billing premium rates, tried delegating the final report write-up to an assistant partner so she could take on more clients. The reports read cleaner and faster. Within six months she lost two of her three anchor accounts. The reports were the artifact clients associated with her sessions, and once that artifact lost her voice, the clients stopped feeling like the work was hers. She now spends an extra ninety minutes per client polishing the final write-up herself, has fewer clients, and revenues are higher. The ninety minutes *was* the product.

## When compromise calls: crowning a trusted deputy

Inevitably you hit capacity. You're doing all five crown-jewel items, Swiss-cheesing everything else, and still there isn't enough of you. That's when you consider crowning a deputy: one person you train to hold one or two crown jewels, with explicit limits and a written promotion ladder.

Deputy delegation is rare and deliberate. The conditions:

- You've worked with the person for at least a year.
- You've documented how you make the crown-jewel decision in question—not the output, the *thinking*.
- You maintain a client escape hatch: any client can request you for a given conversation or decision, and that request is honored without friction.
- The deputy reviews the controversial calls with you for at least six months before they're fully autonomous.

Done well, a deputy doubles your capacity without diluting your trust surface. Done poorly, you've outsourced yourself back into a job. Most solopreneurs never reach this stage and don't need to. If you do, it should feel like succession planning—not like shrinking.

The crown jewels aren't a productivity hack. They're an identity file. Knowing which work is irreplaceably yours clarifies everything downstream: what to delegate, what to refuse, what to price up, and—crucially—what the next chapter of your business actually looks like when you stop being the only pair of hands in it.
