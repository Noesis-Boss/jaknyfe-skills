**[THING 1 — Fix the tone of any email]**

Number one: fix the tone of any email.

We've all written an email we regretted the moment we hit send. Too sharp. Too apologetic. Way too long. Here's the fix — and it's not rewriting from scratch. You already have the draft. Just describe the change you want.

Watch. I'm typing: "Rewrite this email to sound friendly but professional, and about half as long," then pasting the email in.

There. Same message, better manners. Now the part most people miss — you don't have to accept the first answer. Keep going. "Make it warmer." "Now make it firmer — I need a firm deadline." "Cut it by one more sentence." Every reply is another free draft.

One pro tip: end your request with "keep my original meaning." AI rewrites can drift. If you say it out loud, it won't.

**[THING 2 — Turn a brain-dump into a plan]**

Number two: turn your messy thoughts into an actual plan.

You do not need to write instructions perfectly. You don't even need to type. There's a microphone button right there in the box. Click it and ramble for thirty seconds about everything rattling around in your head. I did exactly that for a kitchen backsplash project: need quotes, John can help the weekend after next, still haven't picked tile, budget around eight hundred dollars, and I still need to measure.

Then I typed one line: "Turn this into a checklist with the first thing I should do this week."

Look at that. A real list, in a real order, with the measuring step first — because that's the thing that unblocks everything else. The trick here isn't typing skills. It's that AI doesn't mind a mess. Dump the whole drawer on the counter, then ask it to sort the drawer.

**[THING 3 — Summarize anything long]**

Number three: summarize anything long.

An article. A report. That four-page letter from the insurance company. Paste it in and ask one question: "Summarize this in plain English, and tell me if anything requires me to do something by a deadline."

That deadline half of the question is the difference between a toy and a tool. A summary is nice. Knowing you have fourteen days to respond before they close the claim — that's the reason you'd use this every single week.

One caution before we move on: if it's a document you wouldn't hand to a stranger, don't paste it into a chatbot. I'll come back to that at the end.

**[THING 4 — Decode documents written to confuse you]**

Number four: decode documents that were written to confuse you. Contracts. Terms of service. Medical bills. Warranties. They're long on purpose.

Try this: "Here's my apartment lease. What am I agreeing to that could cost me money? Explain like I'm smart but busy."

I ran this on a real lease before I signed it. In about ten seconds it flagged the auto-renewal clause and the early-termination fee — the two things that actually mattered. Six hundred dollars of fine print, found by reading nothing myself.

Now, let me say this plainly, because this channel doesn't do hype: this is not legal advice, and for anything that really matters, you have a professional confirm it. But as a first pass, it's like having a friend who reads fine print for a living, and works for free.

**[THING 5 — Brainstorm twenty ideas fast]**

Number five: brainstorm when you're stuck.

Here's the mistake almost everyone makes with AI: they ask for one idea. Ask for twenty. "Give me twenty gift ideas for my dad, who fishes and hates clutter, under fifty dollars." Or: "Give me fifteen names for my bookkeeping side business — and don't use the word solutions."

Here's why this works. AI is fast and completely shameless. It'll give you fifteen ideas where a person gives you two, and sure, most of them will be forgettable. But the eighteenth one might be the one you'd never have reached on your own. Your job isn't to accept its ideas. Your job is to react to them. "Make those more practical." "Now go the opposite direction." The reactions are where you win.

**[THING 6 — A proofreader that never gets tired]**

Number six: a proofreader that never gets tired of you.

Anything that leaves your desk — the important email, your resume, the neighborhood newsletter post — paste it in and say: "Check this for grammar, clarity, and anything that could be read the wrong way. Don't rewrite it, just point out problems."

That last sentence is doing the heavy lifting. You keep your voice — it just catches the typo in the first line, and the one sentence that could land badly with half the neighborhood. I run this before anything I actually care about. It's the cheapest editor you will ever hire, and it never sighs when you ask it to check something twice.

**[THING 7 — Rehearse hard conversations]**

Number seven — and this is the one that surprises people. Rehearse a conversation before you have it.

The raise discussion with your boss. The awkward talk with a family member. The price negotiation with a contractor. Type: "I need to ask my boss for a raise. You play my boss — skeptical but fair. Start the conversation."

And it talks back. It pushes the way a real person would push. You stumble here, in practice, so you don't stumble on Tuesday morning. Nobody's watching, and it never gets tired of round two, or round five.

For anything high-stakes — a job interview, a difficult family call — I'd rather do three practice rounds with AI than walk in cold. That's not cheating. Pilots use simulators.

**[REALITY CHECK — on camera, brief]**

Now the sixty-second reality check, because this channel doesn't do hype.

AI gets things wrong. Confidently. It will invent store hours, a statistic, even a clause that was never in your lease — and it'll look exactly like the truth. So the rule stands, and it's the rule I live by: if it matters, verify it. Medical question — ask your doctor. Money question — check the actual source. Anything with a citation — click the citation yourself.

Two more, quickly. Never paste a password, an account number, or anyone's private information into a chatbot — free tools may use what you type. And when an email or a voice message tries to rush you into sending money or a code, slow down and call the person back on a number you already have. You've got more skepticism than the people these scams are designed for. Use it.
